Latest summary

  • The coach should first put themselves in the coachee's shoes; it's fine to feel 'that's me' when doing so.
  • Actions produce results, and in a business setting many actions are conversations; conversations shape culture and productivity.
  • Coaching matters mostly when actions don't lead to results you're happy with, not when things already work.
  • The stance 'I'm unhappy but can do nothing about it' is very rare in practice; there is almost always some option, even if it costs something.
  • New options come from reflecting on how you look at things (the observer you are), which is hard because people often don't see their own 'prison'.

4Four-step coaching process – Step 1: Awareness

  • Awareness underlies the whole process and is very important.
  • Don't assume you already know the coachee, their issue and background, or that you have a full solution.
  • Stay present: it is easy to drift off because of distractions or unwanted thoughts (e.g. being hungry).
  • Keep your senses on what is being said. This will be practised in exercises later.
  • Awareness is not only looking into the mirror. Consider differences between coach and coachee (e.g. an older German man coaching a 22-year-old from India).
  • The coach is judged by the coachee: the collaboration has to feel valuable to them.
  • You need to connect, otherwise nothing will happen. Be aware that sometimes it's not the right match.

5Step 2: Goal – where do you want to go?

  • Similar to consulting: first know where you are, then where to go.
  • A friend simply listens and validates ('I hear you'). Being heard and letting it out is valuable, but that is a friend's role.
  • In coaching you try to move the person out of their current state.
  • After the coachee has talked for a while, ask: 'So? Where do you want to go? Do you want to stand this for the rest of your life?'
  • The coachee may or may not know their goal.
  • Metaphor: after listening to the 'soup' for 30 minutes you understand it, but the coachee shouldn't 'cook in this soup' their whole life.

7Step 2: Goal – setting a realistic goal

  • Example: a graduate finds a great job in Germany. After one year as a junior associate at an international company (about 5,000 employees, subsidiaries in Germany, Europe, Asia and San Francisco), they demand to become the unit lead. For this career stage, that goal is unrealistic.
  • The coach's job is to make sure the coachee sets a realistic goal.
  • Usually the goal is to change a specific situation the coachee is unhappy with, e.g. in the team or with the boss.
  • Ambition itself is not bad, but 'I have to be best in class' may be a goal the coach shouldn't encourage. Ask whether it is realistic, given the competition and the fact that some people are simply smarter.
  • Professor's own example: they were okay at math, but aiming for the best grade in the course would have been setting a goal they could never reach.

8Finding options – from A to B

  • Once the goal is set, look at the gap between where the coachee is (A) and where they want to be (B).
  • Explore several possible routes to reach the goal; there may be many.
  • This is a creative thinking process for change. Prioritise the options afterwards.
  • These steps usually take more than one conversation, but a long session can get this far.
Session 2 · Fri 9 Oct 2026 · 3:58

9Step 4: Creating self-responsibility – from talking to walking

  • Moving from step 3 to step 4 is the hardest part, because it means going from talking to walking ('walk the talk').
  • People usually find they can do much less than they thought they could.
  • The coach makes the coachee feel that self-responsibility is involved and sets a framework that makes it possible.
  • If the coachee only did 30% of the plan by the next session a week later, treat it as 'how come?', not as a defeat or as being a bad person.
  • Make actions as specific as possible. Ask: 'How high is the probability that you do all this within the next seven days?'
  • If the answer is 70–80%, renegotiate to something manageable they are 99% sure to do. Doing what you said beats constantly over-committing.
  • The four steps can guide a coachee over a longer period.
  • Understanding the coachee's view does not mean accepting their story immediately; other people are involved and have their own views. Consider where the coachee comes from and what their setup is.
  • The course will keep coming back to these steps and go deeper each time; so far the focus has mostly been on step 1.

14Requests and promises

  • A request asks for something, e.g. 'I ask you to be on time', 'to participate', 'to send me your presentations one day [before]'.
  • Only three answers to a request should be accepted: yes, no, or 'commit to commit' (I can't answer now, but I'll come back to you by a set time).
  • Don't let people off with 'maybe, let's see'. Instead ask, e.g., 'Would you mind if I call you again next Friday?'
  • If you still get no answer, you know it probably won't go the way you want. Control what you can control; you cannot force a yes.
  • Promises: if you promise something (being on time, being prepared), you should be seen as someone who does what they say.

15Assessments vs. facts

  • We assess all the time ('I like it / don't like it'), especially in business contexts.
  • You can assess things, but you can't deny facts. Extreme example: claiming the Holocaust didn't happen.
  • Fact example: the room has a certain size, and saying it is 4 m² is easy to prove wrong.
  • Assessment example: 'in this room I feel narrow' is personal and cannot be proved wrong the same way.
  • Example: an international student working in a German 'international' company feels like an outsider after a year (colleagues speak German). In the same situation, the student can reframe it: good salary, holidays, can fly home, hobbies and friends in Berlin, so these matter more.
  • Nothing in the situation has changed; only the assessment has. A coach can work on this.
Professor
  • Stressed: You can assess things, but you cannot deny facts. 7:17

16Body

  • The second bucket is body dispositions.
  • Salary negotiation: don't walk in hunched or insecure (you lose before you start), and don't come across as overbearing either. Be stable and prepared.
  • People see you differently when your body posture and your speech acts don't match.

17Emotions and moods

  • The third bucket: emotions and moods (the professor expected students wouldn't name it).
  • The same knowledge and the same situation, in a different emotional state, change what you say and how you say it.
  • Everyone is always in some emotion. Moods are what matter for a coach.
  • The mood is 'the river you are in'. Example: after school you go abroad to study in Berlin/Potsdam, super positive, 'the captain on my boat in the river'.
  • Shifting a mood takes longer than shifting an emotion.
  • Moods define how you come across. This is not esoteric: it goes beyond what is said or written.
Professor
  • Stressed: For a coach, moods (the river you are in) are the interesting part to understand, and they take longer to shift. 14:19

20Actions, results and conversations

  • In coaching you first put yourself in the shoes of the coachee; recognising yourself ('that's me') is fine.
  • You take actions and get results; in business, actions are often conversations.
  • Conversations shape the culture and productivity of an organisation (other factors also play a role).
  • You 'set the tone' (figuratively): even if economic conditions are fine, a toxic environment can undermine results.
  • If your actions lead to results you are happy with, there is nothing to change; coaching is about cases where it doesn't work.
Professor
  • Stressed: In business, actions are mostly conversations, and conversations shape culture and productivity. 0:14
Session 4 · Fri 9 Oct 2026 · 1:36

21'I can't change anything' – a rare situation

  • Typical stance: 'It's not me, I have no influence, I'm unhappy but unable to do anything.'
  • Such situations may exist in theory but are very, very rare.
  • Example: unhappy at work → option to change employer; constraints (just bought a house, family) may make that hard, but you can still change how you see the situation.
  • Accepting 'I can't do anything, so I must suffer until the end of my life' is not something you want.
Professor
  • Stressed: Situations where you truly can't change anything are 'very, very, very, very rare' – there is almost always an option. 2:00

22The observer you are: how you look at things

  • Ask: how can I come up with something that opens new solutions for me?
  • Example: you always wanted to live abroad but have a job you're not really happy with – ask what you are willing to endure to reach the bigger goal you really have.
  • It is hard to reflect on how you yourself look at things.
  • Reference to 'the American fellow in the movie': people sometimes don't see that they are in prison.
  • If you don't like what you have and have tried a lot, develop new options by examining how you look at things; the goal may be 'on the horizon' even if you don't yet know how to get there.
Professor
  • Opinion: When you're stuck, change how you look at things to open up new options; people often don't see they are in a prison. 4:06
