Latest summary

  • Tagged (labelled) equations in LaTeX become clickable links that take the reader back to the page where the equation is.
  • To cite an equation in a paragraph, first give it a \label, then refer to it with the \ref command.
  • Figures, tables and equations all use labels, so the \ref list shows labels for all three (the template already has some figures and tables).
  • Moving an equation in LaTeX renumbers it and updates every reference to it automatically, unlike Microsoft Word.
  • Deleting an equation also deletes its label, so references to it break and show up as question marks.

24Research life cycle

  • The same life cycle applies to your thesis, every research project, and future industry projects 2–3 years from now.
  • 1. Search strategy: choose the topic or identify the problem (in a company: bugs, issues). Build a strategy around the issue and understand it.
  • 2. Developing a proposal: e.g. a thesis proposal, project proposal or feasibility report. It covers how the project will expand and run, technically and commercially.
  • 3. Working on the process: involves all team members, data pipelines, models, etc.
  • 4. Publication: e.g. final project report, thesis report, company report, published paper, or reports for the public, media or a board of directors (the outcomes).
  • 5. Impact: e.g. semester grades, thesis grade, master's coursework, a job promotion, a senior position.

44Video: Definition and examples of quantitative research

  • Quantitative research collects numerical data to identify patterns, relationships or trends.
  • Methods: surveys, experiments and statistical analysis that measure variables and draw conclusions from numbers.
  • Answers questions like 'how much', 'how often' and 'to what extent'.
  • Example 1: a survey of many high school students on weekly homework hours. It gives an average, plus how the hours vary by grade level or school type.
  • Example 2: a fast food chain's customer satisfaction survey sent to hundreds of customers, rated on a 1–10 scale. It shows overall satisfaction and which areas need improvement.
  • Quantitative research fits both examples because it collects numerical data to make broad conclusions about large groups.
Session 13 · Fri 9 Oct 2026 · 1:14

45Strengths of quantitative research

  • 1. Generalisation: findings from many participants can be applied to a broader population, especially when the sample is representative. Ideal for population-wide trends.
  • 2. Objectivity: numerical data and statistical analysis reduce the risk of personal bias. Standardised tools like surveys and tests ensure consistency.
  • 3. Comparability and replication: the structured design makes studies easy to repeat under similar conditions, so results can be verified. This adds reliability and credibility.
  • 4. Identifying patterns or relationships: statistical tools quickly reveal correlations, trends and differences between variables, e.g. income levels vs. education outcomes.
Session 13 · Fri 9 Oct 2026 · 2:32

46Criticisms of quantitative research

  • 1. Lacks depth and context: it can overlook personal, emotional or contextual factors.
  • 2. Overly rigid: fixed questionnaires limit nuanced or unexpected responses, so valuable insights can be missed.
  • 3. Relies on self-reported data: participants may answer inaccurately (memory issues) or dishonestly (wanting to present themselves a certain way), which biases results. The same criticism applies to qualitative research.
  • 4. Misses the complexity of human experience: reducing feelings to numbers oversimplifies them, e.g. a life-satisfaction scale doesn't capture the full range of emotions.
  • These weaknesses are often offset by qualitative research.

48Experimental research and hypothesis testing: bottle-filling example

  • Experimental research is a type of quantitative research that comes with a hypothesis.
  • Example: bottle-filling machines are rated or designed to fill 1000 bottles per hour.
  • This commonly held belief or standard is the null hypothesis.
  • Downtime is when machines are stopped for various reasons: labour, machine, line, utility or maintenance issues. Every minute of downtime affects company profitability.
  • A quality engineer (the researcher) disagrees with the standard. The alternative hypothesis is that the machine does not fill 1000 bottles per hour.
  • The researcher combines experiments and statistics to prove or disprove the null hypothesis, e.g. random samples taken across hours for a week, analysed with statistical tests (the professor said 'p-test, t-test, c-test').
  • Outcome 1: the machine is not performing at 1000 bottles/hour, so the null hypothesis is rejected.
  • Outcome 2: the machine is fine, so we fail to reject the null hypothesis, and the researcher's own hypothesis is rejected.
